## What changes

The earlier explanation dismissed the idea broadly without quantitative
examples. The revision distinguishes routine sorting into fixed sensory styles
from preferences, accessibility and task-appropriate instruction. It explains
the matching comparison, retains a newer positive finding, and qualifies its
practical meaning. It also corrects the classification of the original
teacher-belief survey and removes unsupported generic institutional anchors.

The five evidence summaries distinguish a methodological criterion, a
meta-analysis, a discussion of heterogeneous syntheses, a school-age comparison
and a belief survey. Individual summaries remain unrated. Overall moderate
certainty is an explicitly identified editorial judgment, with no independent
GRADE or formal risk-of-bias assessment claimed.

## Interpretation decisions

The matching criterion requires the best teaching method to differ between
prespecified style groups. A general benefit of one instructional format, or a
correlation between style scores and attainment, does not establish that rule.

The 2024 synthesis is kept as materially relevant positive evidence. Its main
estimate differs slightly from its abstract; both are identified rather than
silently harmonized. Positive signs in two style groups are not equivalent to a
confirmed interaction in every study. The small benefit persisted after excluding
two quasi-experiments, so it is not dismissed solely on that basis. High
heterogeneity, participant exclusions, missing reliability information and short
instructional tasks limit routine classroom conclusions. Other teaching methods'
effect sizes are not ranked against it as if measured in a common trial.

The 2025 discussion explains why constructs and effect measures matter. Its
near-zero matching estimate is not used to cancel the positive 2024 result or
claimed to include that study. The school-age paper's reported interaction
degrees of freedom appear inconsistent with its stated subgroup size; the
revision reports the subgroup and limited conclusion without reproducing the
inconsistent statistic or claiming the issue has been independently resolved.
The 2012 survey's percentages describe its respondents' beliefs only.

Clinton-Lisell and Litzinger report university-foundation support; Rogowsky and
colleagues report NSF support. Both report no commercial/financial conflicts.
Hattie and O'Leary report no competing interests or research funding, with
institutional open-access support. Pashler and Dekker disclosures were not fully
assessed. These are reported disclosures, not an independent conflicts audit.
