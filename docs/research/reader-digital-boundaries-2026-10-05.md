# Digital-security and data-protection coverage

Original-reference check cutoff: 2026-10-05T15:40:09.000Z.

## Distinct additions

The complete 931-review source catalog was searched by title, bottom line,
stable core and editorial summary. All nine existing digital-security reviews
and their account/browsing guides were inspected directly. Sixteen additions
address message-layer versus connection-layer encryption; content versus
metadata; compromised endpoints and conditional recovery; password hashes;
encrypted DNS; DNSSEC; cookie versus other association mechanisms; explicit
cookie removal versus remote records; address/subscriber/device/person
attribution; synchronization versus retained recovery state; writable backups;
file removal and sanitization; at-rest encryption versus runtime access; code
signatures; effective patch deployment; and email-domain authentication.

These are audit-selected gaps, not invented reader submissions. Existing
passkey, code, manager, recovery, expiry, private-window, HTTPS, composition and
VPN reviews retain their identities, content and dates. Neighboring concepts
are not relabeled as additions. Each new body contains 257-280 original words,
excluding shared metadata. Four intentional collections and an original
1,963-word sourced guide connect the questions without adding review counts.

## Evidence and original-source limits

Twenty original references were checked: ten IETF protocol/architecture/network
documents, nine NIST definitions or scoped guidance documents, and the W3C
Privacy Working Group's September 2025 fingerprinting Group Note. The specific
section checks and source limits accompany the citations. These sources are
technical references, not appraised clinical studies, independent security
trials or a measured scientific-agreement poll. Related MLS documents share a
design; NIST references share institutional provenance. W3C working-group
endorsement is not endorsement by W3C or all its Members. Industry and other
institutional contributors are not assumed to be independent product auditors.

The live RFC record showed that RFC 7489 was superseded by RFC 9989 and related
reporting specifications. The actual current DMARC scope was checked directly
in RFC 9989, including its explicit exclusions for content analysis, personal
identity and display-name attacks. RFC 7489 is not a new canonical citation.
RFC 8446 also showed a superseding record; neither that obsolete TLS record
nor its initially inaccessible replacement is used to imply current TLS
recommendations. MLS architecture supplies the needed layer distinction.

Older hash, cookie, network, contingency, storage-encryption and signing
documents are used only for identified definitions and boundaries. Their
historical algorithm, product and configuration examples are not adopted as
current recommendations. The current sanitization citation is the September
2025 final SP 800-88 Revision 2. Its destructive techniques and commands are
not reproduced. Conceptual backup counterexamples do not promise a tested
consumer recovery outcome or prescribe an enterprise schedule to households.

Two Consensus.app discovery searches and two fetched records assisted the
fingerprinting inquiry. The fetched papers were Peter Eckersley's "How Unique
Is Your Web Browser?" and Gomez-Boix, Laperdrix and Baudry's "Hiding in the
Crowd: an Analysis of the Effectiveness of Browser Fingerprinting at Large
Scale." Original EFF retrievals failed, and the university-hosted full text
returned an anti-bot page. Neither generated takeaway nor historical percentage
was adopted as canonical evidence. The accessible original W3C Group Note
supports mechanism distinctions, not a contemporary fingerprint success rate.
No Deep Review was used, and no remaining Consensus account quota is asserted.
Unavailable CISA page/PDF retrievals are not evidence sources or claimed passes.

No passwords, real message content, raw headers, private records or actual
incident data were accessed. There are no attack procedures, code-signing
exploitation steps, tracking implementations, destructive commands, malware
construction, biological procedures or vendor rankings. The explanations
distinguish useful scoped protection from universal guarantees without
claiming that every privacy control is futile.
